⚠️ How to read this honestly

  • Don't accuse anyone with it. No AI detector — free or paid — is reliable enough to be proof.
  • It is biased against non-native English writers. Over 50% of TOEFL essays by real humans get flagged as AI (Liang et al., 2023, Patterns) because simpler, more predictable English reads as "machine-like."
  • It's defeated by paraphrasing and degrades sharply on AI models it wasn't trained on (Dugan et al., RAID, ACL 2024).
  • Best use: a rough self-check on your own writing, and a lesson in why "an AI detector said so" is never enough.
How does this actually work?

This runs a real machine-learning classifier (RoBERTa, trained on the multi-model RAID dataset — so it knows ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and more, not just one) entirely in your browser. It splits your text into sentences and scores each. Three ideas drive AI detection:

Perplexity — how "surprised" a language model is by your next word. AI picks the most predictable word, so its writing is unusually smooth. So does simpler non-native English — which is exactly why the bias above happens.

Burstiness — variation in sentence rhythm. Humans mix long and short sentences; AI trends uniform.

The classifier — a model that weighs these and thousands of subtler patterns into one probability. That number is a correlation, not a fact about who wrote the text.

What it skips — tables of contents, lists of tables/figures, reference lists, headings, and number-heavy rows aren't real prose. They're uniform and predictable, so a detector blindly flags them as "AI" (it'll happily call your List of Tables machine-generated). This tool filters those out and scores only actual sentences — shown faded in the highlight — so the result reflects your writing, not your formatting.

Model: onnx-community/tmr-ai-text-detector (RAID-trained) · transformers.js · nothing leaves your device.
